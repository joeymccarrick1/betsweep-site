How BetSweep picks

BetSweep is a projection model that prices every FanDuel prop. Everything on the site is graded every Tuesday against the box scores, in public, at the odds we showed. This page is what the grades say and how we pick.

Where the numbers come from

Every player gets a projection for the week: yards, catches, touchdowns, fantasy points. It starts from his own season and recent games, then adjusts for the literal matchup — how his offense ranks in that stat against how the opponent's defense ranks at allowing it — the game's expected script (FanDuel's spread and total, which beat our own formula at predicting endings), injuries and who fills in, weather at kickoff, and red-zone and snap usage. The model's chance for a line (say, 60+ receiving yards) is then blended with what FanDuel's price implies, because on the graded legs the raw model ran overconfident when it disagreed with the market. How much weight the model gets depends on the market: the most on touchdowns (about two thirds), where it has added the most; about a quarter to a third on receptions and receiving yards; and very little on passing and rushing yards, where FanDuel's number alone graded better. The weights are re-tested every Tuesday on the newly graded legs.

What three graded weeks taught us

−8%return on every priced leg at FanDuel's price (4,929 legs, Weeks 6–8)
−9 to −12%"floor" legs priced −130 to −400 — they hit 5–9 points under their price
−19%rushing-yard overs, at every line, both sides
+31%plus-money legs (+150 or longer) where the model is 5+ points over the price: 41% hit vs 32% implied

The whole board loses at FanDuel's prices because FanDuel shades its favorites: a short-priced leg taken only because it looks safe is the most expensive thing on the menu. That is the bar a favorite has to clear before it goes in one of our slips. The model's edge shows up at plus money — receptions, receiving yards, passing yards and touchdowns where the book's number is lower than the model's. Longer-priced touchdown scorers paid; touchdown favorites did not. Passing-yard lines above the projection hit 63%; passing floors lost.

So this is how we pick

  • The slips we post are short and built from favorites. About four legs priced around −300 to −200 that multiply to roughly +300. A favorite does not go in because it is a favorite: the matchup, the model's projection and the way we expect the game to go all have to point the same way, and the leg has to pass the builder's risk check. A leg tagged "some risk" or "high risk" stays out.
  • Overs by default, unders when the game calls for them. When a game's "Sweep says" read is a low-scoring one, that game's slip takes unders.
  • Touchdowns in the right spots. A running back in a good matchup, or an either-or leg on a team's top two targets when we already like them for yards and catches.
  • Model's Best is the plus-money list. +150 or longer, model 5+ points over the price, in the markets that have paid. Even-money lines are shown but tracked, not pushed.
  • Long shots are labeled long shots. A long-odds slip like the Monday lottery slip hits rarely, and we say so.
  • Rushing yards. Rushing-yard overs returned −19% in Weeks 6–8. The model's rushing number has since been re-fit on those results and now leans almost entirely on FanDuel's price; a rushing leg has to clear the same checks as everything else.
  • Every pick is frozen before kickoff and graded Tuesday at the price we showed. The receipts and the season page are the whole record — the misses too.
If someone asks "where do the numbers come from?": our own projection model, checked against FanDuel's prices, graded every week in public.

What we don't do

  • We don't call anything a lock. A −300 leg is priced to lose about one time in four, and a four-leg slip at +300 is priced to lose about three times in four.
  • We don't move the model's numbers after a bad week to fit the result. Changes are tested on past weeks first and logged.
  • We don't hide a losing week. The receipt goes up either way.
